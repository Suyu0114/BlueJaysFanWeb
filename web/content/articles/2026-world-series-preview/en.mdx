## A first-time champion, guaranteed

Congratulations to the Brewers and the Rays on reaching the World Series!! And an extra thank-you to the Brewers: sweeping the Dodgers avenged last year's NLCS sweep 🧹 and got some payback for my Jays too 🙏 Now let's dig into this World Series!

On paper, the Brewers are the stronger team. Their 103 wins were the most in baseball, their +214 run differential ranked 1st of 30, and their main hitting and pitching numbers all sit in the top five. The Rays won 98, but their expected record based on runs scored and allowed (x-W/L) is only 90-72, so they won eight more games than their run differential says they should have. They got there with the lineup that strikes out the least in baseball, the pitching staff that walks the fewest, and Drew Rasmussen, who has a 1.29 ERA over 21 postseason innings.

Neither club has ever won a World Series, so this year we're guaranteed a first-time champion 🏆 It's the Brewers' first World Series since 1982 and the Rays' third (after 2008 and 2020).

The two teams met only once in the regular season, a three-game series in Milwaukee from March 30 to April 1, and the Brewers took two. Milwaukee's better record earned home field: Games 1 and 2 are at American Family Field, Games 3 to 5 move to Tropicana Field in St. Petersburg, and if needed, Games 6 and 7 go back to Milwaukee. Both clubs went 55-26 at home, but the Rays were only 43-38 on the road. The Brewers have named Jacob Misiorowski their Game 1 starter; the Rays haven't announced theirs yet.

## Two different ways to win

Both teams win a lot, but in completely different ways: the Brewers get on base and strike hitters out, while the Rays don't strike out and don't walk anyone.

**At the plate: the Brewers are an on-base machine, and the Rays are hard to strike out.**

The Brewers led MLB in OBP (.341), walk rate (10.6%) and batting average (.258), but ranked 30th in HR% (home runs per plate appearance) and 25th in ISO (raw power). They scored 5.14 runs a game, 2nd in baseball, by getting on base and running, not by hitting the ball out.

The Rays had the lowest strikeout rate (18.8%) and the lowest whiff rate (20.7%, swings and misses per swing) in baseball. Their contact quality is weak, though: their Barrel% (balls hit with an ideal mix of exit velocity and launch angle) of 5.7% is the lowest of the 30 clubs, and their xwOBA (the hitting value their quality of contact predicts) of .304 ranks 28th.

**On the mound: the Brewers miss bats, the Rays don't hand out free passes.** Brewers pitchers led MLB in strikeout rate (25.8%) and ranked 2nd in xwOBA allowed (.293): hitters rarely square them up. Rays pitchers were 1st in walk rate (6.9%), WHIP (1.14, baserunners per inning) and BABIP allowed (.263, the share of balls in play that fall for hits), but 25th in Barrel% allowed and 23rd in HR/9.

What stands out is that the Rays' contact quality is weak on both sides of the ball, yet their results beat what Statcast expects. Their hitters' actual wOBA (.320) is .016 above their xwOBA, and their pitchers allowed the lowest BABIP in baseball even though their defense ranked only 15th in OAA (Outs Above Average), so it isn't all the gloves. It's the same story as those eight extra wins: it could be smart usage and positioning, or it could be luck. The Rays' main bats also did go quiet early in the ALCS (3, 3 and 2 runs in the first three games), so it's worth watching in this series.

## Strengths and holes by position

The two lineups are strong in different places. The Rays have the best third base in baseball: Junior Caminero took almost all of the plate appearances there (702, 98%), and his 42 home runs put Tampa Bay's third basemen 1st of 30 in both OPS (.866) and homers. The Brewers ranked 1st in OPS at both first base (.885) and second base (.827): first was mostly NLCS MVP Jake Bauers (.920 OPS while playing first), and Brice Turang held down second almost every day.

Both teams are weakest at shortstop. Rays shortstops posted a .586 OPS, 30th, mostly the glove-first Taylor Walls (wRC+ 74). Brewers shortstops were 26th at .648. And Brewers third basemen hit only 8 home runs all year, the fewest of any club.

## The pitching: the Rays' rotation, the Brewers' bullpen

In the regular season the rotations were close: the Rays' starters had a 3.51 ERA (3rd), the Brewers' a 3.61 (5th), and Milwaukee's starters led MLB in K-BB% (strikeout rate minus walk rate, 19.6%). The real gap was in the bullpen: the Brewers' bullpen FIP (a pitching measure built only on strikeouts, walks and home runs) of 3.69 ranked 4th; the Rays' 4.35 ranked 23rd.

**Misiorowski** was one of the best starters in baseball this year: a 1.80 ERA, with a 99th-percentile xERA and a 100th-percentile fastball velocity on Baseball Savant. He throws his four-seamer 63.4% of the time and gets a whiff on 40.2% of swings against it. But his two postseason starts lasted just 4 and 4⅓ innings, neither reaching five. So how steady he is in this series will go a long way toward shaping how the Brewers use their whole staff. He's starting Game 1.

**Rasmussen** is the Rays' ace: three postseason starts of 8, 6 and 7 innings, 21 innings in all with a 1.29 ERA, going deep every time. His pitch mix is balanced, with the cutter, four-seamer and sinker each making up 20 to 30 percent. Fun fact: the Brewers drafted him (sixth round, 2018), then traded him to Tampa Bay in the deal for Willy Adames.

**Peralta** had an ordinary regular season (4.42 ERA), then threw 16⅔ postseason innings with a 1.08 ERA and took home ALCS MVP. (He pitched three times in the ALCS, starting twice; calling him this year's Mr. October isn't a stretch.) He pitched for the Brewers from 2018 to 2025, was traded to the Mets last winter and then to the Rays before the deadline, and now he faces his old team.

In the postseason, the Brewers' bullpen has mattered even more: 40⅔ innings in eight games, more than the starters' 30⅓, with a 1.33 ERA and a 0.89 WHIP. The Rays are the opposite: their starters carried them with a 1.98 ERA over 50 innings, while the bullpen has a 3.60 ERA in 30. Rays closer Bryan Baker saved 41 games with a 1.44 ERA in the regular season and hasn't allowed a run in seven postseason innings. ESPN rates the Rays as baseball's best staff in high-leverage spots this season, which doesn't contradict that 23rd-ranked bullpen FIP: their best arms are saved for the biggest moments.

## How they got here: pitching first

Both teams pitched their way to the World Series.

The Brewers allowed 21 runs in eight games and just 3 home runs in 71 innings.

The Rays allowed 23 runs in nine games, with a 1.01 WHIP.

At the plate, the Brewers scored 4.25 runs a game, while the Rays' on-base percentage is only .293.

The Brewers beat the Padres 3-1 in the NLDS, then swept the Dodgers in the NLCS. MLB.com points out that Milwaukee trailed at some point in all four of those games, and the Dodgers hit just one home run in the series. The Rays swept the Yankees in the ALDS, allowing only 5 runs in three games; in the ALCS they were down 2 games to 1 against the Guardians, then won three straight.

Runs are always scarcer in October: per MLB.com, teams have averaged 3.5 runs a game this postseason, against 4.48 in the regular season. So the Brewers' 4.25 is actually pretty good. Still, these are only eight or nine games, so treat the rates as a guide, nothing more.

## My pick

I think the Brewers will win, for two main reasons:

- **The Brewers' bullpen is genuinely steady and strong.** It ranked 4th in FIP in the regular season, and this postseason it has thrown 40⅔ innings in eight games with a 1.33 ERA. Even the Dodgers couldn't break through: 11 runs in four NLCS games.
- **The Rays' offense is still relatively inconsistent.** Their postseason on-base percentage is .293, they scored 8 runs combined in the first three ALCS games, and their regular-season contact quality ranked near the bottom of the league.

If the Rays are going to win, the key is wearing down the Brewers' bullpen. Milwaukee's starters don't go deep anyway (under four innings a start this postseason), so games get handed to the bullpen early, and if the Dodgers couldn't solve that group, the odds of the Rays beating it head-on aren't great. The more realistic script: Rays starters go six or seven innings the way Rasmussen has, keeping their own weaker bullpen out of games, while the Brewers' relievers have to work every day. Abner Uribe pitched in each of the first seven postseason games and gave up 2 runs in his last outing. Draining that bullpen and dragging the series to Game 7 is probably the Rays' winning formula. But the Brewers have had seven days off since the NLCS, so their bullpen starts the series fresh, and that plan would only pay off late in the series.

ESPN's 10,000 simulations also have the Brewers ahead, 59% to 41%. But anything can happen in a best-of-seven: the Brewers themselves just swept the two-time defending champion Dodgers.

Still, I'm hoping this one goes six or seven games, haha!
